Verdict: Strong (11/14, close to Excellent)

The paper offers substantial support for standardization in the areas where it documents existing practice, prior art, and coordination problems, but its case is thinnest when it must explain why the standard itself—rather than a library or vendor extension—needs to take the action, and that weakness leaves the core rationale asserted more than demonstrated.

- The paper is strongest in showing that two active committee proposals now answer the same configuration question inconsistently, and that both cannot be adopted without specifying one in terms of the other.
- The record of a decade of shipped named-guarantee checking across three vendors gives the paper solid implementation experience grounding for the general shape it discusses.
- The paper's use of public code search and vendor-reported line counts establishes who is affected by the competing models with concrete evidence.
- The most glaring omission is the lack of an established argument for why a standard mechanism is necessary when the evidence largely shows shipping practice already works through libraries and compiler flags without unified standard semantics.
