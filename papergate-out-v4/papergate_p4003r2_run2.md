Verdict: Strong (10/14)

The paper’s strongest material is comparative and experiential: it points to prior art, a two-project implementation across three platforms, and a companion rationale document that carries the design analysis. What remains thin is the affirmative case for standardization itself—several passages gesture at why the protocol earns its place in the standard, but too often the argument is asserted rather than demonstrated, with the same brief slogans reused rather than developed. The affected-party and coordination sections are especially under-supported: the intended users are named, and interoperability is claimed, but the paper does not show who depends on this becoming a standard facility or how it would fit with the existing work beyond broad compatibility statements.

- The paper clearly establishes prior art and design context, including a companion paper covering alternatives and implementation projects on three platforms.
- The implementation experience section is the most concrete body of evidence, with named projects, platforms, and maintainers.
- The case for affected users is thin: a protocol rationale and a small benchmark table do not establish who needs this in the standard or what they cannot do today.
- The most glaring omission is a sustained argument that standardization, rather than a common library protocol, is necessary; the supporting passages mostly revisit the same “small protocol, big rewards” claim without showing what only the standard can provide.
