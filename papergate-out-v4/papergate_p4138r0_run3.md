Verdict: Adequate (6/14)

The paper does make a credible showing on the narrow question of how implementations actually behave, and it gestures toward the historical context behind the current wording, but it leaves several foundational parts of the standardization case largely unargued. The thinnest support concerns the basic justification for changing the standard: it does not establish who is affected, why the standard is the right place to address the problem, or what alternatives were seriously considered.

- The most substantive support is the implementation evidence, since the paper cites compiler exploration showing agreement on most of the cases in question and notes the specific divergence in Clang.
- The paper also gives some background on how the current rules arose, though it stops short of turning that history into a demonstrated need for a standard change.
- The comparisons with existing practice are offered as prior art, but the paper does not develop them into an argument that the status quo is stable or that the proposed direction has precedent worth following.
- The most glaring omission is the absence of any established account of who is affected and why the standard, rather than a library or educational guidance, should resolve the issue.
