Verdict: Weak (3/14, close to Adequate)

The paper gives a clear account of why the grammar’s readability and accessibility problems matter, but it leaves the bulk of the standardization case unstated, including who would actually be affected and why any of this requires a standards change rather than an editorial convention. The strongest material concerns motivation and the chosen syntax, while the thinnest concerns evidence of need, implementation experience, and fit with the existing standard’s machinery.

- The paper establishes that the current grammar’s lack of grouping and repetition creates verbosity, recursion, and accessibility problems that the proposal would address.
- It offers some discussion of prior art and alternative notations but does not establish that those alternatives were evaluated in a way that shows the proposed approach is preferable for standardization.
- It does not establish who is affected by the current grammar’s limitations, which makes the practical urgency of the proposal hard to assess.
- It provides no evidence for implementation experience, coordination with other parts of the standard, or why a library or purely editorial solution could not achieve the same goal.
