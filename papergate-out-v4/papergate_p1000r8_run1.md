Verdict: Weak (2/14)

The paper offers only preliminary, high-level assertions about the value of its proposed scheduling model, leaving nearly every burden of justification unmet. Its strongest statements concern general dissatisfaction with past uncertainty and perceived improvements over the last decade, but these are presented as claims rather than supported arguments, and the thinnest areas are those most directly bearing on standardization: the need for a standard, coordination, the inapplicability of a library solution, and implementation experience.

- The most substantive support is the assertion that the current model has existed since 2012 and that the authors do not want to return to an earlier approach, which at least signals continuity with prior practice.
- The paper gestures at affected parties by citing reduced defect reports and review comments, but this is a bare claim without evidence tying the reduction to the proposed change.
- The discussion of why the proposal matters rests on broad statements about marketplace uncertainty and compiler fragmentation, with no substantiation connecting those conditions to the specific remedy being proposed.
- The paper is silent on why this requires standardization at all, how it would interoperate with existing processes, why a library or non-normative mechanism would not suffice, and whether any implementation experience exists.
