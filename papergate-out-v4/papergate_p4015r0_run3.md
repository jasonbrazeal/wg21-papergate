Verdict: Adequate (4/14)

The paper’s strongest case rests on a genuine and well-illustrated problem: enforcement choices becoming part of a contract can create surprising, dangerous incompatibilities, and the burden of specifying enforcement ought not to fall silently on declarations. Beyond that motivating scenario, however, the proposal offers little evidence that its direction is necessary, workable, or preferable to alternatives. The claims about prior art, suitability for standardization, coordination, and feasibility are asserted rather than supported, while the affected audience, library-only solutions, and implementation experience are left effectively unaddressed.

- The paper clearly establishes that mixing enforcement with contract conditions can lead to backward-incompatible surprises and serious security exposure.
- Its claim that existing alternatives foundered is mentioned but not substantiated with concrete comparison or analysis.
- The argument for why this belongs in the standard rather than a library is asserted through design philosophy, without showing a real gap.
- The paper offers no evidence about who needs this feature or whether any implementation experience exists, making the practical case for standardization hard to judge.
