Verdict: Adequate (6/14)

The paper gives real texture to the problem it wants to solve, but much of the evidence for its specific remedy rests on one person’s meeting experience and loose analogies rather than a demonstrated need for a standing rule. The case is strongest when it describes how in-meeting changes can quietly move the basis of consensus, and thinnest when it must explain why this belongs in formal process rather than in chairing practice or working-group habit.

- The paper convincingly establishes why the issue matters, especially the point that national body experts may not have seen text that later becomes the basis for a recorded tally.
- It gestures toward alleviating the burden on reviewers and toward consistency with WG14 scheduling, but the prior art and alternatives are asserted rather than developed into a clear argument for this particular mechanism.
- The assertion that recorded tallies are the right boundary depends on claims about later sessions using counted results as evidence, but that downstream effect is claimed rather than established.
- The paper offers no argument for why the standard is the necessary instrument, nor any account of implementation experience that would show the proposed rule works in practice.
