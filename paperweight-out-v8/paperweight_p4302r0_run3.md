Verdict: Adequate (5/14)

The paper makes a credible case that the committee’s current scheduling practices create real, avoidable costs, and it grounds that argument in concrete procedural history and existing rules. The support is thinnest where the paper needs to show that the proposed change belongs in the standard itself, that it can be implemented in practice, and that the affected parties actually experience the problem as described.

- The strongest support is the paper’s explanation of why the issue matters, particularly its account of how judgment-based deadlines waste the committee’s prepared attention and impose unintended asymmetries.
- The paper also does well in engaging with prior art and alternatives, acknowledging competing proposals and tracing its lineage to P2138R4 while arguing for a brighter-line mechanism.
- The claim about who is affected rests almost entirely on one author’s anecdotal contrast between two meetings, which is suggestive but not enough to establish the breadth of the problem.
- The most glaring omission is the absence of any implementation experience or evidence that the proposed rule can be operationalized, alongside an unaddressed question of why a standard change, rather than a library or process-level fix, is necessary.
